Many of the owners we speak to bought their property in Crete with the same idea: enjoy it for a few weeks a year and rent it out the rest of the time. It is a good plan. But once the first season starts, the reality of running a rental from another country becomes clear: guest messages at 2 a.m., a cleaner who cancels on a changeover day, a pool pump that fails in August.

Here is what we have learned about making it work.

## 1. Decide what kind of rental it is

Before anything else, decide how the property will be used. A sea-view villa with a pool, a two-bedroom apartment in Chania's old town and a village house in the hills attract different guests, at different prices, in different months. Your choices about furnishing, minimum stays, pricing and even photography follow from that positioning.

Be honest about your own use too. If you want the whole of August for your family, plan your pricing and marketing around the remaining months from the start.

## 2. Get the paperwork right first

In Greece, a property used for short-term rental must be registered and receive a registration number (known as the AMA), which must appear on every listing. Rental income is taxable, and there are additional per-night charges and reporting obligations that change from time to time. Safety requirements, such as fire extinguishers, a first-aid kit and appropriate insurance, also apply.

The details depend on your situation, so work with a Greek accountant from day one. A good manager will coordinate with your accountant, but should not be your tax adviser.

## 3. Build the local team before you need it

The single biggest risk for an absent owner is not having reliable people on the ground. At a minimum you need:

- **Housekeeping** that can turn the property around in a few hours, with spare capacity in peak weeks.
- **Laundry and linen** in sufficient sets so that one late delivery does not delay a check-in.
- **A handyman and specialist technicians** for plumbing, electrics and air-conditioning.
- **Pool and garden care** on a fixed schedule during the season.
- **Someone who can meet guests** and solve problems in person.

Whether you assemble this yourself or work with a management company, insist on clear standards and checklists.

## 4. Present the property properly

Guests decide in seconds, based on the first photo. Professional photography, an honest description and well-chosen amenities pay for themselves quickly. Small details matter: good mattresses, blackout curtains, strong Wi-Fi, a well-equipped kitchen and clear instructions.

## 5. Price with the calendar, not against it

Demand in Crete changes sharply between May, August and October. A single price for the whole season leaves money on the table in July and empty nights in May. Review prices regularly against demand, local events and comparable properties, and adjust minimum stays to avoid unsellable gaps.

## 6. Keep score

Ask for, or keep, a simple monthly statement: nights booked, gross income, platform commissions, cleaning, consumables, repairs and the net result. Over two or three seasons, these numbers tell you what is working and where the property needs investment.

## 7. Plan for the off-season

An empty property in winter needs attention: humidity, leaks after storms, garden growth and security. Schedule regular checks and use the quiet months for maintenance and improvements, so the property opens in spring ready for guests.

## The bottom line

A holiday rental in Crete can be rewarding, but it is a small hospitality business, not passive income. Set it up properly, with clear responsibilities, reliable local people and transparent reporting, and it can run smoothly even when you are thousands of kilometres away.
